The challenge

How might we offer a valuable financial alternative to people who are dissatisfied with the current formal financial system?

My approach

I led qualitative research to understand why people opt out of the formal financial system — mapping their money habits, informal alternatives, and moments of distrust.

Six out of ten Peruvians sit outside the formal financial system (INEI, 2023) — not because they lack financial needs, but because they don't trust the institutions meant to serve them. Banks are seen as bureaucratic, rigid, and quick to say no.
Who we were designing for

We focused on the underbanked: people who technically hold a bank account but rely heavily on informal financial networks — family loans, community savings groups, cash — to actually meet their needs. On paper, they're "included." In practice, the formal system isn't working for them.

Insights
Trust is the real currency

People are open to new ways of banking, but only from institutions with an established, credible reputation.

Rigidity is the deal-breaker

Formal financial products don't flex the way people's real lives do. The informal networks people already rely on succeed precisely because they do adapt.

Independent workers are an overlooked, sizeable opportunity

Peru has 7.8 million independent workers (INEI, 2017) who are actively dissatisfied with traditional banking, have real economic capacity and are already comfortable with digital tools.

The Solution

We designed Freelea, a platform that connects freelancers with job opportunities while creating a verified record of completed work and payments. By building a trusted financial history, freelancers can strengthen their credibility and gain access to credit and other financial opportunities traditionally out of reach.

Freelea — social media ad

Outcomes & reflections
Outcomes

Freelea validated a real, sizeable need and won early internal support, but it was shut down after roughly a year. The concept depended on a two-sided model — freelancers on one side, employers posting verified work on the other — and no marketing team was ever allocated to build the employer side of the platform. Without that supply of verified jobs and payments, the financial-history engine that gave the product its value couldn't function, and the project was ultimately killed.

Reflections

The sharpest lesson from this project was that financial inclusion isn't a product problem first — it's a trust problem. The most elegant platform in the world doesn't matter if it hasn't earned the same confidence people place in their informal networks.
